What the model leaned on

Compared with the average person in the survey (). A pattern in the data, not advice.

    • Everyone in the survey is an American born 1980–84.
    • “Partner” means married or living together.
    • The model is right about groups far more often than about one person.

    What the data shows

    In points: going from 40% to 45% is +5 points. Each finding compares people who are otherwise alike.

    1. Age matters most. The chance rises steeply until about 30, then levels off. Being single at 25 is ordinary: about half of 25–29-year-olds are.
    2. For men, work matters most. Going from no job to a full-time job goes with about +22 points, at every age. For women after 25 it goes slightly the other way.
    3. Pay matters more for men. From $20k to $60k a year: +11 points for men, +3.5 for women. Part of this runs backwards: having a partner raises earnings.
    4. College delays partnering rather than preventing it. Graduates are behind in their twenties and ahead by their late thirties.
    5. Personality counts a little. Outgoing, organised and calm people are partnered more often: about +3 to +5 points each, for men and women alike.
    6. Height and weight matter little. 170 → 185 cm goes with about +1 point for men. Heavier people are partnered more often, but mostly because weight goes up after settling down.

    What it can’t tell you

    • What causes what. These are patterns, not proven effects.
    • Partners you don’t live with. They count as single here.
    • Other generations or countries. Younger people partner later and meet differently.
    • You. A 70% means 3 in 10 people like you were single.

    Built from three public surveys: NLSY97, NHANES and HCMST 2017. Method and code on GitHub (MIT).
